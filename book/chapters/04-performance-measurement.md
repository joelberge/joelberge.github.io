# Chapter 4: Performance Measurement

**Part:** Part 2 — Pruning the Branches
**Tree element:** The branches — first cut.

## Premise

One of the most important problems in business is that we do not actually know how performance is distributed across products or customers. This requires matching benefits with costs — which is not easy in a multi-product or service organization, where many costs are indirect and invariant in the short run. Economics conveniently assumes access to a clean marginal cost; this chapter shows that this is a simplification, and that measurement itself requires choices.

## Content to cover

- Why cost allocation is a *measurement problem*, not a solved technical fact — economics' marginal-cost assumption is a simplification, not a given.
- **Full costing:** the principle that all costs should be covered by products/customers.
- **Contribution-based costing:** only variable costs are allocated; fixed costs are left out.
- The chapter's real point: both of the above skip the deeper question — *what actually causes costs to vary*, and *over what time frame is variability even the relevant question*?
- Introduce the theoretical principle of **allocating based on cost causality** — a system for tracing costs along causal chains rather than mechanically applying full or contribution costing.
- Explicitly frame the takeaway as a *principle*, not a technique: the specific allocation techniques (ABC, TDABC, etc.) are deferred to Chapter 5.

## Key frameworks & references

- Full (absorption) costing vs. contribution/variable costing
- Cost causality / causal-chain allocation principle
- Cost behavior and the relevant time horizon for variability

## Connections

- **Builds on:** Chapter 3's problem-structuring toolkit (common-size analysis, MECE) applied here to the cost side specifically.
- **Sets up:** Chapter 5, which introduces the concrete techniques (ABC/TDABC) that operationalize the cost-causality principle.

## Writing guidelines (for the author)

- Keep this chapter about the *principle*, not the *technique* — explicitly resist introducing ABC/TDABC here even though they're the natural next thought; that's Chapter 5's job and the two chapters should read as principle-then-method.
- Make the "economics assumes marginal cost, reality doesn't hand it to you" point early and vividly — it's the chapter's hook and justifies why measurement choices matter at all.
- Give full costing and contribution costing roughly equal, fair treatment before pivoting to why both are incomplete — don't strawman either.
- The time-horizon question (what's fixed/variable depends on the window you're looking through) deserves its own clear sub-section; it's easy to gloss over and is conceptually the hardest part of the chapter.
- End by naming cost causality as the thread Chapter 5 will pick up with real techniques.
