# Chapter 3: Problem Identification and Structuring

**Part:** Part 1 — The Forest and Its Trees
**Tree element:** The arborist's toolkit — how to examine the tree before treating it.

## Premise

Any good arborist analyzes the tree before starting treatment. The same is true for firms: before jumping to solutions, we need to understand the basic structure of the problem. This chapter is a methodological bridge — it hands the reader a toolkit for structuring a business problem, which the rest of the book (starting with Part 2) will put to use.

## Content to cover

- **Common-size analysis and the "Willie Sutton rule":** how to identify a firm's cost structure and locate where the most important aspects of the business lie — "go where the money is."
- Once the big-ticket items are identified, the need for structured thinking: **decision trees** and the **MECE principle** (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) as the discipline for breaking a problem into clean, non-overlapping, complete parts.
- Frame this explicitly as a general-purpose toolkit (borrowed from strategy-consulting practice) rather than something specific to costing or pricing — it will be reused across Parts 2–4.
- Close with a short worked example: take a messy, real-sounding business problem and show common-sizing + MECE structuring turning it into a decision tree, without yet solving it.

## Key frameworks & references

- Common-size financial statement analysis
- The "Willie Sutton rule" (go where the money is)
- MECE principle (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive)
- Decision trees as a structuring device

## Connections

- **Builds on:** Chapter 2's closing point that operationalization is the missing piece.
- **Sets up:** Part 2, which is the first place this problem-structuring toolkit gets applied to a real analytical question (cost allocation).

## Writing guidelines (for the author)

- This chapter is explicitly a *toolkit* chapter, not a theory chapter — keep it practical, with the worked example doing most of the teaching.
- The Willie Sutton anecdote (bank robber, asked why he robbed banks: "because that's where the money is") is a good hook — use it, but keep it brief and get to common-size analysis quickly.
- MECE should be taught as a discipline with a clear test the reader can apply ("could two branches of my decision tree both be true at once?" / "have I left anything out?"), not just defined in the abstract.
- Keep the worked example realistic but simple — one firm, one clear cost-structure surprise, structured but not solved (the solving happens once we're inside Part 2's specific techniques).
- Signal clearly at the end that this toolkit will resurface — it should not feel like a one-off detour.
