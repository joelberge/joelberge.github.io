# Chapter 2: The DNA of the Tree

**Part:** Part 1 — The Forest and Its Trees
**Tree element:** The tree's own genetic makeup — what makes this particular tree different from its neighbors in the same forest.

## Premise

Chapter 1 established what we can expect *on average* in a given forest. This chapter asks: does this particular tree have above-average profitability potential within its industry, or not? We introduce three competing (but ultimately complementary) hypotheses for why some firms are more profitable than others.

## Content to cover

- **Hypothesis 1 — Customer selection:** some firms simply serve more profitable customers than others. Include a graph showing potential variation given different market positions (flag as a candidate figure).
- **Hypothesis 2 — Activities:** some firms perform more valuable activities than others, and these are hard to imitate. Porter's position-based / activity-based view of competitive advantage.
- **Hypothesis 3 — Resources:** some firms hold more valuable resources than others, and these are hard to imitate — the resource-based view (Barney, 1991).
- The synthesis: competitive advantage most often comes from the *combination* of all three — aligning activities with resources in a mutually reinforcing way that compounds over time. This is the chapter's real payoff, not any one hypothesis alone.
- Explicitly flag that this book will not go deep into strategy theory for its own sake — the point is that *operationalizing* strategic resources and activities is what matters, which is the bridge into Part 2's operational analysis.

## Key frameworks & references

- Porter (activity-based/positioning view)
- Barney (1991), resource-based view (note: chapter brief referred to "Barnes" — likely Barney; confirm before drafting)

## Connections

- **Builds on:** Chapter 1's industry-average potential.
- **Sets up:** Part 2 (operational analysis) — the chapter should end by naming "operationalization" as the missing piece, handed off explicitly to Chapter 3 and Part 2.

## Writing guidelines (for the author)

- Structure as three named hypotheses in sequence, each getting roughly equal weight, before the synthesis — resist the temptation to declare a "winner" early.
- Keep the resource-based view and activity-based view at a conceptual, applied level (what makes a resource/activity hard to imitate: rarity, causal ambiguity, path dependence) rather than a full literature review — this is a popular-science book, not a strategy-theory monograph.
- The customer-selection hypothesis (1) is the least conventionally "textbook" of the three — make sure it gets a genuinely concrete illustration (a graph/segment example), not just a mention, so it doesn't read as an afterthought next to Porter and Barney.
- The closing synthesis paragraph is the most important paragraph in the chapter: make the "alignment of activities and resources compounding over time" idea vivid, since it is the conceptual seed for the entire operational-analysis part that follows.
- Do not let this chapter balloon into a full strategy course — the brief explicitly says not to go deep; keep it as a firm, confident overview.
