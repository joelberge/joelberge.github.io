# Chapter 1: The Forest Surrounding the Profit Tree

**Part:** Part 1 — The Forest and Its Trees
**Tree element:** The forest (industry/environment) around the trunk.

## Premise

No firm is an island. A firm is situated in a setting — its industry and competitive environment — much as a tree is situated in a forest. What we can expect from a tree depends heavily on whether it stands in a dense forest, competing for light and nutrients, or in an open field. This chapter is about establishing what we can expect — on average — from a firm given the "forest" it is planted in, before we say anything about the specific tree.

## Content to cover

- The core question: what is the *average potential profitability* in an industry, and why does it vary so much across industries?
- Porter's Five Forces as the primary framework for structuring industry attractiveness analysis (rivalry, buyer/supplier power, threat of entry, threat of substitutes).
- The profitability tree framework from Lien & Knudsen (2016) as a complementary/alternative lens — introduce it explicitly as a named framework, not folded into Porter's.
- Illustrate with contrasting industries (dense forest vs. open field) so the "average potential" concept is concrete before any firm-specific analysis begins.
- Close by making explicit that this chapter only establishes the *industry* benchmark — the firm-specific question ("is this particular tree better or worse than the forest average?") is deliberately deferred to Chapter 2.

## Key frameworks & references

- Porter's Five Forces
- Lien & Knudsen (2016), profitability tree / industry analysis framework

## Connections

- **Builds on:** the Introduction's potential-vs-performance framing.
- **Sets up:** Chapter 2, which moves from industry-average potential to firm-specific potential (why some trees in the same forest do better than others).

## Writing guidelines (for the author)

- Keep this chapter at the industry level of analysis throughout — resist pulling in firm-specific resource/activity arguments; that is Chapter 2's job and the two chapters should feel like a clean two-step (industry, then firm).
- Use one or two real or realistic industry contrasts (e.g., a commoditized vs. a differentiated industry) to make "average potential profitability" tangible; a simple chart of profitability dispersion across industries would help here (flag as a candidate figure).
- Present Porter's Five Forces at teaching depth (define each force, give one example per force) rather than assuming prior exposure.
- Introduce Lien & Knudsen's tree explicitly by name and citation — do not blend it silently into Porter's model; briefly note how the two frameworks relate/complement rather than compete.
- End with a clear forward pointer to Chapter 2 so the "average vs. specific" distinction is explicit to the reader.
