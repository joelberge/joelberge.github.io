# Chapter 13: Pricing for Humans — Not Robots

**Part:** Part 4 — The Root System
**Tree element:** The roots meeting the berries — the human root of pricing.

## Premise

This chapter brings behavioral elements into pricing: how people actually think about prices, and why that matters for companies. Firms can exploit these biases, or think more holistically about pricing — explicitly referring back to Chapter 8's holistic-pricing framing.

## Content to cover

- Key behavioral pricing phenomena: reference-price effects, price framing, anchoring, loss aversion in pricing perception, fairness perceptions — chosen and scoped to what's needed to make the strategic point, not an exhaustive behavioral-economics survey.
- The dual path explicitly: firms *can* exploit these biases for short-term gain, or integrate behavioral realism into a holistic pricing strategy (link back directly to Chapter 8) for more durable value capture and customer trust.
- Connect to Chapter 12's ethics frame: exploiting pricing psychology is a live example of the "boundary condition vs. utility function" tension raised there — this chapter should feel like a worked application of Chapter 12's argument, not a separate topic.
- Close by asking the reader to revisit Chapter 8's pricing methods (cost/market/customer-based) through this behavioral lens: how does knowing customers aren't perfectly rational change how each method should be applied?

## Key frameworks & references

- Behavioral pricing effects (reference price, anchoring, framing, fairness perception, loss aversion)
- Explicit callback to Chapter 8 (Holistic Pricing) and Chapter 12 (Ethics and Strategy)

## Connections

- **Builds on:** Chapter 8 (pricing methods) and Chapter 12 (ethics framing), which this chapter deliberately synthesizes.
- **Sets up:** Chapter 14, which shifts the behavioral lens from customers to employees.

## Writing guidelines (for the author)

- Curate the behavioral-pricing content tightly — pick 3–4 phenomena that clearly connect to the exploit-vs-integrate argument, rather than cataloguing the full behavioral-economics pricing literature.
- Make the "exploit or integrate" choice the chapter's spine, with concrete contrasting examples of each (a pricing practice that clearly exploits vs. one that clearly respects customer psychology while still being commercially sound).
- The explicit callback to Chapter 8 should not just be a mention — actually revisit one or two of Chapter 8's three pricing methods and show how the behavioral lens changes their application.
- Keep the ethics connection concrete (short, specific tie-back to Ch. 12's boundary-condition-vs-utility-function framing) rather than restating the whole ethics argument.
