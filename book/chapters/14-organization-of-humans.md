# Chapter 14: Organization of Humans

**Part:** Part 4 — The Root System
**Tree element:** The roots — the human system that sustains the tree from within.

## Premise

This chapter introduces behavioral elements concerning motivating employees and getting them to make good decisions. It is not always just a matter of getting people the right information — it is about understanding how humans actually make decisions inside organizations.

## Content to cover

- Why information alone doesn't guarantee good decisions: introduce the gap between having the right data (as taught throughout Parts 2–3) and organizational members actually acting on it well.
- Core behavioral/organizational themes: motivation (intrinsic vs. extrinsic), incentive design pitfalls (e.g., measurement gaming, misaligned KPIs), and cognitive/organizational biases in managerial decision-making.
- Connect explicitly back to Parts 2 and 3: all the costing, pricing, and profitability tools taught so far are only as good as the humans applying and acting on them — this chapter is about that human layer.
- Keep scope disciplined: this is an organizational-behavior primer in service of the book's profitability argument, not a standalone OB textbook chapter.

## Key frameworks & references

- Motivation theory (intrinsic/extrinsic), incentive design and gaming/KPI pitfalls
- Behavioral decision-making biases relevant to managerial contexts

## Connections

- **Builds on:** the implicit assumption throughout Parts 2–3 that measurement leads to good decisions — this chapter makes that assumption visible and complicates it.
- **Sets up:** Chapter 15, which extends the same "who/what makes the decision" question to AI and automation.

## Writing guidelines (for the author)

- Open by explicitly naming the assumption this chapter complicates: "so far, we've assumed that if you measure it right, people will act on it right" — then show why that's not automatic.
- Use one or two vivid, realistic incentive-gaming examples (a KPI that got gamed, a well-intentioned metric that produced a perverse outcome) to ground the abstract organizational-behavior material.
- Keep the motivation-theory material applied and brief — a popular-science treatment (recognizable concepts, memorable examples) rather than an academic OB survey.
- Explicitly bridge to Chapter 15 at the end: having covered how humans alone behave, the next chapter asks what changes when decisions are shared with AI/robots.
